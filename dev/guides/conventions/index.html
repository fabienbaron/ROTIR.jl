Conventions

Coordinate systems, angles and signs used by the geometry, orbit, radial-velocity and plotting code.

Sky plane

Astronomical sky-plane convention:

AxisDirectionInternal name
xEast (left on plots)-proj_west
yNorth (up on plots)proj_north
ztoward the observernormal_z > 0 is visible

Projected vertices are stored as proj_west and proj_north, in mas. Plots negate the West component so East is on the left.

Stellar surface coordinates

The unit sphere uses spherical coordinates:

CoordinateSymbolRangeDefinition
Colatitudeθ0 to πfrom the North pole
Longitudeφ0 to 2πincreasing eastward

Cartesian: x = sin(θ)cos(φ), y = sin(θ)sin(φ), z = cos(θ).

Spin-axis angles

Inclination

Angle between the spin axis and the line of sight (+z, toward the observer).

ValueMeaning
0°pole-on
90°equator-on

Position angle (PA)

Angle of the projected spin axis on the sky, from North through East (counterclockwise on plots).

ValueProjected pole points ...
0°North
90°East

Spin-axis direction:

spin_x = -sin(inc) sin(PA)   (West component)
spin_y =  sin(inc) cos(PA)   (North component)
spin_z =  cos(inc)           (toward observer)

Rotation matrix

ZXZ Euler angles, applied as a right multiplication of the vertex coordinates:

xyz_rotated = xyz · R(ψ, inc, PA)

where R = Rz(-ψ) · Rx(inc) · Rz(-PA) and the angles are:

AngleSymbolMeaning
ψ2π (t − t0) / rotation_periodrotation phase
incinclination (degrees in star_params)tilt from the line of sight
PAposition_angle (degrees in star_params)orientation on the sky

Rotation is counterclockwise on sky plots seen from the visible pole: a surface feature moves West → North → East as time increases. (rot_vertex in src/geometry.jl.)

Rotation sense

Stellar spin and orbital motion have the same sense: counterclockwise on the sky when the angular momentum points toward the observer.

MotionFace-on (inc or i = 0°)On sky (East left, North up)
Stellar spinFeature: West → North → EastCounterclockwise
Orbital (increasing ν)Secondary: North → East → SouthCounterclockwise

A spin feature at (West=1, North=0) at t=0 is at (West=0, North=1) at t=P/4; a secondary at (North=1, East=0) at ν=0 is at (North=0, East=1) at ν=π/2.

Rotation phase and the epoch t0

The surface spins at

\[\psi = 2\pi\,(t - t_0) / P_\mathrm{rot}\]

with t0 and rotation_period in the units of the epochs. t0 defaults to 0.

What ψ = 0 means on the surface

  • The sub-observer longitude is 270° at ψ = 0 and decreases with phase:

    \[\lambda_\mathrm{sub-obs} = 270° - 360°\,(t - t_0)/P_\mathrm{rot}\]

    where longitude is atan2(y, x) of the body-frame tessel centre, in [0°, 360°);

  • The body +x axis lies in the plane of the sky. For surface_type = 1 that axis is radius_x, so for a prolate (tidally distorted) figure ψ = 0 is a maximum-elongation phase.

Setting t0 from a published ephemeris

For a tidally locked binary the long axis points at the companion:

At t = t0
long axisin the plane of the sky
orbital positionnodal passage
projected areamaximum
ellipsoidal light curvemaximum

A published time of nodal passage (a maximum of the ellipsoidal light curve) can be used as t0; the map is then in that paper's longitude system.

Two nodal passages per orbit

A prolate silhouette repeats every half rotation: the long axis is in the plane of the sky at ψ = 0 and at ψ = 0.5. Which end of the tidal axis faces the companion (ascending or descending node) is set by the ephemeris's definition. If it is unknown, t0 is ambiguous by half a period; resolve it with the surface map or the radial velocities.

`t0` here is not the orbital `T0`

The orbital T0 (ORBIT_ELEMENTS, src/orbits.jl) is the time of periastron: M = n·(t − T0). A circular-orbit spectroscopic ephemeris quotes a nodal passage instead; the two are not interchangeable. The surface t0 is the rotation phase zero, independent of both.

Visibility

A surface element is visible when its outward normal has z > 0. The mask is soft:

weight = sigmoid(κ · normal_z)

κ sets the sharpness of the limb transition (create_star(…; κ = 50)). Pixels with weight ≤ 0.01 are dropped.

Orbital output frame

binary_orbit_rel and binary_orbit_abs return positions in the observer frame:

ComponentDirection
xNorth (Dec increasing)
yEast (RA increasing)
zaway from the observer (positive = receding)

The orbital z axis points away from the observer; the stellar-geometry z axis points toward it. orbit_to_rotir_offset converts the orbital position to the sky-plane offset:

offset_west  = -(East component)    # West = -East
offset_north =   North component

Radial velocities

binary_RV returns radial velocities in the units of K1, K2 and γ (km/s by convention):

SignMeaning
positivereceding
negativeapproaching
Vrad = γ + K [cos(ν + ω) + e cos(ω)]

where ν is the true anomaly. The secondary uses ω, the primary ω + π.
