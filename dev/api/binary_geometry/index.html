Binary geometry: two stars in one frame

create_star orients a star with rotate_star: a spin at rotation_period about a fixed sky axis. src/binary_geometry.jl instead derives one body→sky rotation from the orbit and applies it to both components, so a Roche lobe's tidal bulge follows the companion and each surface element's position relative to the companion is known (needed for mutual irradiation).

Frames

FrameAxes
ROTIR sky(1) West, (2) North, (3) toward the observer; right-handed
src/orbits.jlx = North, y = East, z = receding
Binary co-rotatingx̂ = primary→secondary, ẑ = orbital angular momentum, ŷ = ẑ × x̂

sky_of_orbit(x, y, z) = (-y, x, -z) maps the second frame to the first. It is orbit_to_rotir_offset plus the line-of-sight component.

Both lobes share one rotation

compute_potential_primary puts the companion at +D x̂; compute_potential_secondary puts it at −D x̂ (solve_R_L1 uses θ = −π/2 for the secondary). The primary's body +x̂ therefore points at the secondary and the secondary's body +x̂ points away from the primary, which is the same direction in space. Both spin about the orbital angular momentum, so one 3×3 matrix orients both components.

Times are JD. ROTIR stores vertices as row vectors, so the rotation is applied as xyz * transpose(R).

create_binary_star differs from create_star in two places: D comes from the shared orbit, and the orientation is R instead of the spin rotation. The rest (normals, soft visibility, projection, ldmap, setup_oi!, polyft) is the shared finish_star.

center_offsets

create_binary_geometry stores the secondary's sky-frame offset (West, North, toward observer; mas) in star2.center_offsets, and sets star1.center_offsets = [0,0,0]. handle_reflection and the animation code use this field to place the two meshes. The vertex arrays stay centred on their own component, so polyft and binary_phase_shift are unaffected.

Volume-conserving Roche shape

FunctionDescription
tessel_solid_angles(tessels)Solid angle of each tessel on the unit sphere, normalised so Σ = 4π
roche_mesh_volume(tessels, star_params, D, Ω; secondary)V = (1/3) Σ rᵢ³ ΔΩᵢ on the mesh, in units of a³
roche_omega_for_volume(tessels, star_params, D; secondary, V_target, D_ref)Ω giving the same volume at D as at D_ref
roche_omega_table(tessels, star_params, bparams; secondary, npoints)Linear interpolant Ω(D) over [1−e, 1+e]

By default update_roche_radii holds rpole fixed and lets Ω follow D, so the volume changes over an eccentric orbit. volume_conserving = true holds the volume fixed instead (PHOEBE re-solves Ω from a fixed equivalent-volume radius). The difference between the two is the ellipsoidal variation. Pass volume_conserving = true, or precompute a table and pass omega:

Ωtab = roche_omega_table(tes1, params1, bparams; secondary=false)
D = compute_separation(bparams, t)
star = create_binary_star(tes1, params1, bparams, t; omega=Ωtab(D))

roche_mesh_volume is the volume of the mesh ROTIR integrates over, not the analytic volume, so both sides of V(Ω) = V_target use the same discretisation.

Reference phase: ROTIR and ELISa conserve the volume the star has at D = a (D_ref = 1); Wilson–Devinney and PHOEBE legacy use periastron (D = 1 − e); PHOEBE 2's requiv is phase-independent. The choice changes the absolute size slightly, not the modulation. Pass D_ref = 1 - e to roche_omega_for_volume for the WD convention.

demos/spica_binary_roche.jl holds the polar radius fixed (fillout_factor_primary = -1); demos/spica_irradiation_movie.jl and demos/spica_proximity_movie.jl use volume_conserving = true.

Mutual occultation

FunctionDescription

binary_cvis sums the two components without occultation by default. Pass occultation = true (or :exact, :soft) to apply it, or a precomputed (w1, w2) to reuse it across a scan:

v2, t3a, t3p = binary_observables(tm1, star1, tm2, star2, data, phase; occultation=true)

The components are treated as disjoint convex bodies, as in the radiosity solver: the one whose centre is nearer the observer is in front everywhere, and only the far star is masked. A tessel of the far star is hidden where it falls inside the near star's projected outline, which costs O(n₁+n₂).

Two methods:

  • method = :exact (default): the fraction of each far tessel's projected quad outside the near component's convex hull, by Sutherland–Hodgman clipping (convex_hull_2d → polygon_convex_clip_area). No free parameters. The clip requires a convex polygon; the projection of a convex body is convex.
  • method = :soft: the tessel centre is tested against the azimuthal projected_limb_profile, smoothed by wᵢ = σ((dᵢ − R_limb(φᵢ))/(width·R_limb(φᵢ))), with width = softness (default √(4π/npix)/2). Needs no convexity and is C∞, for use under differentiation.

At nside 4/3, :exact takes 1.15 ms and :soft 0.22 ms. Only tessels straddling the limb are clipped; the others are classified by a circle test against the hull's inscribed and circumscribed radii. A bounding-circle test on the two components returns (nothing, nothing, 0) without allocating when the disks do not overlap.

For Spica (R₁ = 7.40 R☉, R₂ = 3.74 R☉, a = 25.5 R☉) the minimum projected separation is 0.594 mas against R₁+R₂ = 0.673 mas: a grazing eclipse, consistent with the MOST photometry (Desmet et al. 2009, reported in Aufdenberg et al. 2015). At closest approach the secondary hides about 2.3 % of the primary's projected flux, 1.9 % of the system total.

Apsidal motion and period change

omega_at(bparams, tepoch) returns the argument of periapsis at tepoch, ω(t) = ω₀ + dω·(t − T0), with dω in degrees/day (positive in the direction of orbital motion). binary_orbit_rel, binary_orbit_abs, binary_RV, binary_proj_plane and binary_frame use it. For Spica (U ≈ 139 yr, dω/dt ≈ 2.6°/yr) ω advances about 21° across the 2007–2015 CHARA observations, which moves the predicted secondary position by up to 0.44 mas (MIRC precision: 0.01–0.05 mas).

compute_eccentric_anomaly applies dP (Ṗ, days/day; positive = lengthening) through the quadratic ephemeris t_n = T0 + P·n + ½·Ṗ·P·n², for either sign of Ṗ. At β Lyrae's rate, neglecting Ṗ is worth 65° of orbital phase after 10⁴ d. If the extrapolated period would reach zero within the epoch range, it warns and uses a constant period.

Synchronicity

synchronicity(p) = p.P / p.rotation_period is F = ω_rot/ω_orb, the ratio of angular rates. It is PHOEBE's syncpar, ELISa's synchronicity and P in RocheLobe.f90, and enters the centrifugal term as ½F²(1+q)r²(1−ν²).

For Spica's primary (v sin i = 161 km/s, F ≈ 1.92) the reciprocal rotation_period/P would scale the centrifugal term by F⁴ ≈ 13.6 and give 0.5 % rotational flattening instead of 9.4 %. Checked against libphoebe.roche_Omega(q, F, d, [x,y,z]) at F = 0.521, 1.0 and 1.92.

The Spica demos set rotation_period = P_orb (synchronous). Spica's measured rotation corresponds to rotation_period = P/1.92 for the primary and P/1.65 for the secondary.

The tidal term for an eccentric orbit

The frame is centred on the star. That origin accelerates toward the companion at G·M₂/D², so the frame carries a uniform pseudo-force with potential −G·M₂·x/D²; in units of G·M₁/a:

    −q·x/D²  =  −q·r·λ/D²

This term is translational and assumes nothing about the frame's rotation. The rotational term is the centrifugal potential about the star's own spin axis, ½F²(1+q)r²(1−ν²), with F referenced to the mean orbital rate, so it has no D dependence. Sepinsky, Willems & Kalogera (2007, ApJ 660, 1624) derive this frame for nonsynchronous eccentric binaries.

A centre-of-mass origin, with the linear term written ω²·x_cm·x and x_cm = D·q/(1+q), would also require the rotational coefficient ω² = (1+q)/D³, which is D-dependent; neither ROTIR nor the reference codes do this. ((1+q)/D³ is the circular rate at separation D; the instantaneous orbital rate is larger by 1 + e·cos ν.)

Aufdenberg et al. 2015 (eqs. A18/A27/A30) give −q·r·λ·D, which differs by D³. PHOEBE 2 and ELISa both use −q·r·λ/D² and a D-independent rotational term:

PHOEBE 2  phoebe/lib/gen_roche.h
    Ω = 1/ρ + q[(δ² + ρ² − 2ρλδ)^(−1/2) − ρλ/δ²] + ½F²(1+q)ρ²(1−ν²)

ELISa     elisa/binary_system/model.py, pre_calculate_for_potential_value_primary
    b = d²;  c = 2·d·cs;  d_coef = q·cs/b;  e = ½·F²·(1+q)·sin²θ
    Psi1 = 1/r + q/√(b + r² − c·r) − d_coef·r + e·r²          (cs = λ, d = D)

With the ·D form, the term exceeds the tidal attraction beyond a threshold separation and points the bulge away from the companion. For Spica (q = 0.619, rpole/a = 0.290) the threshold is D/a = 1.031; the published eccentricities (0.065, 0.118, 0.123) all put apastron beyond it. Radii toward (λ = +1) and away from (λ = −1) the companion:

  D/a     front(λ=+1)  back(λ=−1)   difference
                                                 −q·r·λ·D:
  0.90     0.31953      0.29892      +0.0206   toward
  1.00     0.30661      0.30268      +0.0039   toward
  1.04     0.30302      0.30413      −0.0011   away
  1.12     0.29734      0.30692      −0.0096   away
                                                 −q·r·λ/D² (ROTIR):
  0.90     0.31173      0.30502      +0.0067   toward
  1.12     0.30318      0.30088      +0.0023   toward

The form also sets the amplitude of the ellipsoidal variation, which is the observable fitted for the apsidal constant. For a circular orbit (D ≡ 1) the two forms agree.

Tests in test/test_reflection.jl:

  • "tidal bulge tracks the companion": the bulge is the longest radius at every orbital phase;
  • "Roche potential: gravity is the gradient of the potential": compute_gravity_* equals |∇Ω| of compute_potential_* by finite differences;
  • "Roche potential reduces to the D ≡ 1 reference": rescaling to units of the instantaneous separation (r = D·s, then multiplying by D) reproduces RocheLobe.f90's potential.

roche_volume and roche_area

Both integrate over the equipotential in (μ, φ) by Romberg quadrature, using the μ- and φ-symmetries, and are tested against a direct mesh sum and the sphere limit.

  • The radius in each direction is a Halley solve seeded from roche_polar_radius, the minimum radius on the equipotential, so the iteration converges to the lobe surface and not to a root beyond the L1 saddle.
  • roche_area includes the projection factor dA = r² dΩ / cos γ, with cos γ = |∂Ω/∂r| / |∇Ω|.
